# Job Description Writing Rules — The 10x Method

Source: *The 10x Method* (Hexa), Chapter V — "Hiring and Retaining Early Talent", pages 120-123.

## The core problem the book calls out

Most startup JDs are either too vague/quirky (trying to sound "cool", confusing candidates) or copy corporate language that misses the early-stage reality. A JD isn't a formality — it should excite the right candidates and filter out the wrong ones. Be clear about the mission, honest about the challenges, specific about what success looks like. Highlight the level of ownership early hires will have — that's what great early candidates are looking for.

## The 7 sections, with the book's exact guidance for each

**1. Title**
Choose a title people actually search for, not just one that sounds attractive. E.g. "Founding Engineer" is fine as a title, but include "Software Engineer" in the body so candidates find the role via common keyword searches.

**2. About the company**
Your company is new and unknown — this section is crucial. Show what makes you unique and innovative. Early hires join startups because they care about the "why". Start with a punchy paragraph on what you're building and why it matters. Emphasize how first employees will shape culture and impact.

**3. What you'll do (missions)**
Forget the laundry list of responsibilities. Describe the core problems they'll own — early hires need to know what's broken and what they'll be trusted to fix, not every bullet point spelled out.
- Example phrasing: "You'll be responsible for developing and maintaining scalable front-end and back-end solutions."
- Communicate how the role will evolve: e.g. "This role starts as an individual contributor, but within 6–9 months, we expect you to help hire and mentor additional team members."
- Consider adding a "What success looks like in 6 months" section.

**4. Technical stack (for certain roles)**
List the stack — common for engineering, but also worth including for GTM, marketing, ops roles. If undefined, say the employee will help define it. Add a line like "Don't hesitate to apply even if you're not familiar with every tool in our stack" to widen the candidate pool.

**5. Who you are (requirements)**
Common founder mistake: too many requirements relative to the missions section (because requirements are the "easy part" to write). Rules:
- Highlight just 3-5 traits or experiences that really matter.
- Clarify nice-to-have vs. essential.
- Keep it short — longer requirement lists measurably reduce the number of women who apply.

**6. Why join [company]**
List values and benefits — even if values aren't formally defined yet, describe real ways of working (async-first vs. meeting-heavy, team rituals, etc.). Beyond classic perks (health insurance, snacks), highlight the unique impact opportunity early-stage roles offer.

**7. Hiring timeline**
Outline the process clearly so candidates know what to expect. Suggested 4-step process from the book:
1. Initial call — quick intro with the CEO/other founder
2. Technical interview — discussion with the CTO about experience and skills
3. Meet the team — casual conversation with a current team member for cultural fit
4. Reference calls — final step before offer; at least two reference calls (one former colleague, one former manager); offer the candidate the chance to speak with people who've worked with your team too.

## Related notes (worth surfacing to the founder)

- **The first interview is about convincing, not evaluating.** The candidate might not be sold on an unknown company — your job is to make them want in before you assess them. At the end of an outbound call: "Do you want to be part of this process?" Only move into evaluation mode once they've opted in.
- **Follow-ups matter.** Quick check-ins between interview stages (even a WhatsApp thread) keep the relationship warm, help you track if they're interviewing elsewhere, and gauge motivation.
