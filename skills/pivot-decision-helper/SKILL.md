# Pivot Decision Helper

Source: *The 10x Method* (Hexa), Chapter IV — "Building the MVP", pages 86-90. Section: "I launched my MVP, now what?"

## Step 1: Focus on one metric (p.87)

Tempting to measure every corner of the product — resist it. Pick a single **focus metric** that can only move when the business grows in a healthy way, and use it as a compass (this doesn't mean tracking only one metric — several are valuable — but there should be one north star). For B2B software, the book suggests picking from:

- **Customer retention rate** — % of users returning after a set period. Retention is everything early on; a strong rate signals real ongoing value, a low rate (especially design partners dropping off) is a red flag.
- **MRR growth** — monetizing the MVP early, even minimally, is one of the strongest PMF signals; if design partners are willing to pay for a minimal product, that's a very positive indicator.
- **Active users** (daily/weekly) — reflects real engagement and stickiness, unlike vanity metrics like total sign-ups.

## Step 2: But don't spend too much time on it either (p.88)

Early-stage product development should be driven by conviction and qualitative feedback, not excessive data analysis — talking to users beats getting lost in dashboards. Instead of obsessing over metrics: build a product *you'd* want to use, watch session recordings, talk to users every day, and use analytics to validate (not dictate) decisions.

## Step 3: Should I pivot? (p.88-90)

Revisit the book's own startup-idea framework (see `idea-categorizer`): a strong idea rests on 3 components — **problem, solution, and GTM strategy**. If any one of them falters (problem isn't painful enough, solution doesn't resonate, or GTM strategy isn't delivering / doesn't scale), a pivot may be the right move. Importantly: you can have a real problem *and* a solution users love, and still need to pivot because your **GTM or business model** doesn't capture enough value — that was the case for Collective, who pivoted post-seed from a hybrid SaaS/service model to a social-network-style platform for freelancers, after realizing their service-heavy model couldn't scale past €10M and was stretching the team between two conflicting operating models (services vs. tech R&D).

**Extra signal worth using** (Simo Lemhandez, Folk): combine qualitative (surveys like "how disappointed would you be if you could no longer use this?", frequent user conversations) with engagement ratios (WAU/MAU, DAU/MAU — calibrated to what "high usage" should mean for the specific product category). The most useful emotional signal isn't indifference vs. love — it's that **users who are frustrated or even a bit angry** are often easier to convert into champions than lukewarm (5-6/10) users are into true fans (9-10/10). Indifference, not hate, is the real enemy of PMF.

## Workflow

1. **Identify or confirm the one focus metric** for this product, from the 3 options above (retention / MRR growth / active users) or a category-appropriate equivalent — narrow to one north star even if others are tracked too.
2. **Check signal quality**: how much real data/time has passed — early numbers are noisy; don't overreact to day-one data.
3. **Diagnose against the 3 components** (problem / solution / GTM) — ask which one specifically seems to be faltering, using the founder's own description of what isn't working.
4. **Apply the emotional signal check**: are early users indifferent (bad sign) or frustrated/opinionated (better sign, means they see potential)?
5. **Give a direct verdict**: too early to tell (what more signal is needed, by when) / tweak one component, don't pivot fully / real pivot signal — and if it's the latter, name which of the 3 components is the actual root cause, since a GTM/business-model pivot looks very different from a product pivot.

## Output format

1. Focus metric being tracked + current signal quality
2. Diagnosis across problem / solution / GTM — which one (if any) is faltering, and why
3. Emotional signal read (indifference vs. frustration from early users)
4. Direct verdict: too early / tweak / pivot — with the specific reasoning
